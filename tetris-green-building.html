Tetris on the Green Building

Building-Scale Light Installation • PCB Design • Design for Manufacture • Thermal Management

I'm so grateful to have been a part of the amazing team that brought this project back to life. I did a lot of sourcing and assembly for this project in addition to learning PCB design and assembly, design for manufacture, thermal management, new machining techniques, and a ton more, plus generally just having a great time!

The Project

Recently I worked on an amazing team to complete a joint passion project: a building-scale light installation hardware venture that pushed me past small, self-contained prototypes and into ‘mass’ production. It introduced me to scaling up electronics for large, collaborative systems; integrating my machining experience into a more complex, multifaceted project than I had previously done; designing custom PCBs for the first time; and more. Mentored by some amazing friends, I learned to design electronics that remain robust, debuggable, and manufacturable when multiple subsystems and people are involved, with our team iterating through four revisions of the MCU board and two of the LED board before locking in a design that could survive being reproduced 180 times over.

In addition to being replicable, it was also designed and built to be a permanent installation meant to last for decades. On one end this meant we had to think about avoiding environmental damage across every single module, seriously taking into account sealing, moisture, and thermal stress in a way my past projects never demanded. On the other end, we had to think about documentation. The team turns over on a minimum four year cycle as students graduate, so anything I designed needed to be understandable and maintainable by people who are many steps removed from the original decision makers. And while I was familiar with the discipline of writing for a future team, this task was definitely complicated by the fact that I was learning to understand the system as I was building it!

Moving from a single working prototype to a couple hundred identical units also changed how I thought about tolerances and failure modes; even a small per-unit defect rate meant real issues once multiplied across every module in the field, with several bodges coming into play. But in the end, it all came together. Standing with the team on the night of our self-imposed deadline (after weeks of very late nights and maybe a few missed classes), seeing an entire building light up in multi-colored patterns on command was one of the most rewarding engineering experiences I've ever had.

Want to learn more? Check out this great blog post (thanks, Ellie!) on the return of the hack, and the write-up of the original 2012 hack. We completely redesigned everything from scratch in order to revive this iconic MIT hack.
