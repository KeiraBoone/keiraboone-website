CERBERUZ: NASA LunaRecycle Challenge

Materials Testing & Characterization Lead • Injection Molding • Mold Machining • Digital Twin • Sept 2025 to present

Watching nanoscale voids that I never would have caught with the naked eye show up under the SEM always makes me grin, even when they are a likely source of the unfavorable results I just got on the mechanical tester. That was for CERBERUZ, MIT's NASA LunaRecycle Challenge team and first place winner out of more than 1,200 registered teams, in both the Prototype and Digital Twin divisions.

The system recycles lunar trash (reclosable bags, drink pouches, rehydratable pouches, Zotek F30 foam, and nitrile gloves) into parts a crew could actually use: screwdriver handles, hydroponic net pots, pipe fittings, and 3D printing filament. It does this with composite injection molding, grinding everything together and treating the non-thermoplastics as reinforcing filler inside a thermoplastic matrix. None of these filler and matrix combinations had known mechanical properties going in, so as materials testing and characterization lead, I got to build that understanding from scratch. I also worked across preprocessing, machining our molds, running the injection molder, and the digital twin.

The System

Rather than requiring clean, sorted material, CERBERUZ embraces the mess. Mixed logistics waste goes through one mechanical pipeline and comes out as composite feedstock. The system uses no water and no consumables, and any unused material can be reprocessed, so material loss stays near zero.

HERCULES

A double-shaft shredder that reduces large or flexible items to sub-centimeter pieces. We cut and shredded by hand for the demonstration, so this stage was designed but not built.

CHARON

A centrifugal mill with an interchangeable sieve (up to 18,000 rpm) followed by a ribbon mixer that blends matrix and filler by mass ratio into molding-ready powder.

PERSEPHONE

A modified commercial tabletop electric injection molder. With custom molds it makes parts, and with a filament guide and spooler added it extrudes 3D printing filament, so one machine does both jobs. Average cycle time is about 75 seconds, or roughly 48 parts per hour.

DEIMOS

Our digital twin: a machine model, a mold fill model, a filament extrusion model, and a machine-learning quality predictor, all driven from one MATLAB and Simulink interface.

Prototype numbers: about 220 kg total mass, 1.6 kW peak power (from PERSEPHONE), and a commercial-parts bill of materials of roughly $23,700. A demonstration batch is 33 molded products plus 3 filament samples, about 0.57 kg, from five of the eight challenge waste items.

Characterizing the Feedstock

Particle size, and dropping liquid nitrogen

The original plan embrittled Zotek with liquid nitrogen before grinding. I characterized particle size distributions on the ground feedstock by laser diffraction and compared nitrile, aluminum-laminate drink pouch, and Zotek ground with and without liquid nitrogen. Dominant peaks sat around 800 to 920 µm and held 79 to 93% of the volume. Liquid nitrogen only added a small fine population (about 4% by volume near 33 µm) and did not move the main peak, and the coarser powder still fed, melted, and injected fine. That let us cut liquid nitrogen out of preprocessing entirely: same usable particle size, one less consumable, and one less cryogenic hazard.

One honest wrinkle: the mill sieve is 80 µm, but laser diffraction reports a volume-equivalent diameter, so thin flakes pass the mesh and still read much larger. That is useful to know when interpreting the numbers.

Thermal stability of Zotek F30

Zotek can release hydrofluoric acid when overheated, so processing temperature limits matter. NASA rules cap it at 250 °C, with HF release reported above about 290 °C. We sent neat Zotek F30 foam to Eurofins EAG for TGA with evolved-gas FTIR, run in both argon and air up to 800 °C. Mass was flat to roughly 400 °C, and our molding setpoints of 240 to 270 °C sat at least 130 °C below the measured onset. HF showed up in both atmospheres, so running in vacuum or inert gas removes combustion products but not HF. Two sweep points did exceed the 250 °C cap, and nitrile blends at 260 °C are above the literature onset range for nitrile (210 to 245 °C) and remain untested.

What filler does to the process window

Higher solids loading raises melt viscosity, which means a hotter barrel and longer hold to keep the cavity packed while the gate is open. Swapping filler also changed the window. Switching from Zotek to nitrile gloves pushed the required barrel temperature up by as much as about 15 °C and, at 30% loading, lengthened the hold by 8 seconds, even though the two ground to a similar particle size (about 865 and 903 µm). That is the kind of thing that seems obvious, but you can only prove and understand it by actually characterizing each feedstock.

When a material just does not work

Nomex and cotton were a lesson in scoping. Cotton would need a barrel hotter than its own degradation temperature to avoid short shots, and once we dropped liquid nitrogen we could no longer embrittle Nomex, so long strands clogged both the mill and the molder. We descoped from seven eligible waste items to five and tuned each remaining blend individually.

Mechanical Testing

I molded dogbone specimens for every blend and tested them to ASTM D638 (Type IV-like specimens, 5 mm/min, strain from a video extensometer, five specimens per composition). The data went into a master sheet that grew to 102 rows, 56 of them complete, and fed our quality-prediction model.

What the data taught me about my own measurements

The most useful results were sometimes the ones that told me my method was off, and I tried to treat them that way.

  • Yield definition: I applied the 0.2% offset over too wide a strain range. These strain-hardening semicrystalline blends have no sharp yield point (our milestone HDPE read 2.7 MPa yield against a 7.7 MPa ultimate strength), so I am re-extracting yield per D638 and reporting the offset explicitly.
  • Crosshead is not gauge: a quick elastic finite element model of the dogbone showed gauge strain is about 1.24 times crosshead strain, so crosshead-based modulus reads roughly 19% low. An extensometer fixes it.
  • Hardness dropped: Vickers readings varied from 2.3 to 3.4 HV within a single dogbone, which points to viscoelastic recovery of the indent and surface preparation. Tensile data plus density are more informative here.
  • Voids and CT: I used CT scans and ImageJ to estimate void area percent, but the voxel size turned out too coarse for the voids and 2D thresholding is biased, so I set those numbers aside. Next steps are a density method against the rule of mixtures and higher-resolution micro-CT and SEM cross-sections.

Net result: the trends and rankings are credible, while absolute yield values stay provisional until the D638 re-analysis is done.

Molds, Machining & Filament

Product molds were designed in CAD, programmed in CAM, and machined from 6061 aluminum on a HAAS CNC mill. Because the composites are more viscous than plain polymer, I used larger runners and gates, and every new mold geometry meant re-checking the optimized parameters.

  • Screwdriver: insert molding with a metal blade placed in the mold before injection, so the plastic flows around it and can transmit torque.
  • Pipe: a hollow interior that cannot be machined into the mold directly, so I used a removable side pull with a 1° draft angle to release it through shrinkage.
  • Net pot: thin ribs and varying thickness, a good test of fill on complex geometry.

Filament extrusion

PERSEPHONE's filament mode swaps the mold for a die and a guide, tensioning, and spooling stage, tuned on HDPE pellets first and then on reclosable bag feedstock. A quality check on a 20.2 m strand of reclosable bag filament, sampling 20 locations in two perpendicular axes, measured an average diameter of 1.35 mm with a 0.11 mm tolerance and an average eccentricity of 0.18.

Digital Twin (DEIMOS)

Our digital twin pulls real data from the machine, the power meter, the Instron, and the CT scanner into one pipeline and uses it to simulate and predict. My characterization data is what trains its quality-prediction model.

  • Machine model: a Stateflow and Simulink state machine of the injection cycle. On a nominal run it matched recorded maximum pressure within 1%, clamp force within 1%, injected volume within about 5%, and cycle time within about 10%.
  • Mold fill model: a finite-volume viscosity, temperature, pressure, and velocity model that flags frozen gates, short shots, and flash.
  • Filament model: a one-dimensional enthalpy model that predicts solidification distance, final diameter, and sag.
  • Quality prediction: one Gaussian process regression per output, trained on 45 samples and tested on 11, predicting modulus, yield, and strength with 95% confidence intervals. Held-out R² was 0.91 for Young's modulus and 0.85 for ultimate strength, but only 0.50 for yield, the output most hurt by the offset problem above.

The twin talks to the real machine in both directions, so predicted settings can be pushed back to the molder for the next cycle. Its limits are documented too: warpage and flash are threshold flags rather than deformation predictions, and the screwdriver and net pot need a 3D extension of the thin-cavity model.
