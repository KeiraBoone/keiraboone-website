MEMS Tunable Diffraction Grating

6.2600 Micro/Nano Processing Final Project • MIT.nano • Electrostatic Comb-Drive Actuation

A low-cost, voltage-tunable optical diffraction grating built on a silicon-on-insulator wafer with a single photolithography step. Two electrostatic comb drives pull a flexible grating apart to change its pitch, steering the diffracted beam with no moving optics. This was a three-person project: I am so grateful to have worked with Jieruei Chang and Alysha Rawji, who were absolutely amazing collaborators from first sketch to last cleanroom run.

Project Summary

Beam steering usually relies on mechanically rotating optics, which limits switching speed and makes systems bulky. A tunable diffraction grating changes the optical response itself, so light can be steered without rotating anything. Applications include compact spectrometers for CubeSats, laser modulation, and high-speed optical communications.

Our goal was to make the fabrication as simple as possible: one mask, one DRIE etch, one release, one metallization. We designed the device, fabricated it in MIT.nano, wirebonded it to a carrier PCB, and measured its optical response to an applied voltage with a HeNe laser and a position-sensitive photodiode.

Design & Theory

Device concept

A suspended flexible diffraction grating (6µm bars on a 12µm pitch, giving a ~50% duty cycle for best efficiency) is held by flexures and actuated by two comb drives, one on each side. With no voltage the grating sits at its minimum pitch; at high voltage the comb drives pull it apart to its maximum pitch.

Flexure sizing

The flexures have to resist sagging under gravity so the suspended structure never collapses onto the silicon below. Modeling eight parallel flexure springs against a 2µm maximum allowable displacement gave a theoretical maximum flexure length of about 1930µm at 4µm width. To leave margin for lateral pull-in instability, we designed 300µm flexures. In the actuation direction that gives roughly 0.325 N/m per flexure and about 1.54 N/m for the full assembly.

Comb drive

Modeling the comb drive as a line of parallel-plate capacitors, two comb drives with 100 fingers, 3µm spacing, and 10 V produce about 0.295 µN, a theoretical DC deflection of 0.192 µm.

Optics

For a 633 nm HeNe laser, that displacement corresponds to a theoretical optical response of about 16.9 microradians, governed by the grating equation. Diffraction efficiency is maximized by keeping the bar width near half the pitch, which is why we chose 6µm bars on a 12µm pitch.

Fabrication Process

Built on a 4-inch SOI wafer with a 10µm device layer and a 3.5µm buried oxide, with the full layout generated parametrically in Python.

1. Pattern

Plasma ash, HMDS prime, then spin 5µm of AZ10XT photoresist at 6000 rpm (chosen for its high viscosity and steep sidewalls in thick layers). Expose on the MLA150 maskless aligner, develop, and hard-bake.

2. DRIE

Etch 10µm through the device layer on the SPTS Pegasus to expose the buried oxide, then strip the resist by ashing. The 4-inch wafer was temporarily bonded to a 6-inch carrier because of tool size constraints.

3. Release

Cleave into chips and run a buffered HF etch for about 1 hr 15 min to undercut roughly 5µm of oxide, freeing small structures. The pattern is designed so every suspended surface is within 5µm of an initially exposed region.

4. Critical point drying

Conventional drying would destroy the devices through surface tension, so chips are rinsed in DI water, moved into isopropanol, and critical point dried.

5. Metallization

Deposit 20 nm Ti and 200 nm Au by e-beam evaporation at 45° angles to form the comb-drive parallel-plate capacitors and electrical contacts.

6. Packaging

Attach chips to an SOIC-28 carrier PCB, wirebond the device pads to the PCB pads, and solder on header pins.

Testing & Results

A 633 nm HeNe laser was focused through a 500 mm lens onto the grating while a function generator drove the comb drives. A position-sensitive photodiode (PSD) tracked the first-order diffraction spot.

  • Deflection was visible at 5 V and increased with voltage, following theory surprisingly well. The PSD was uncalibrated, so the conversion to lateral shift is an estimate.
  • Actuation was lower than predicted, which we attribute to DRIE overetch (below). The device failed while driving at 9 V.
  • The grating's own movement was too small to see directly, as expected, but unintended diffraction from the comb drive showed clear motion between the two actuation extremes.
  • The first-order peak sat at 61 mm, corresponding to β ≈ 3.27° against an expected 3.05°.

What Went Wrong (and What We Learned)

A big part of the project was characterizing why so few devices survived. We documented each failure mode and its mitigation:

  • DRIE undercut and negative sidewalls: SEM at 45° showed up to ~2µm of undercut, likely from the footing effect on SOI, where charging of the insulating oxide redirects ions into the sidewalls. On 3µm comb fingers this was enough to detach fingers early and shrink the force-generating overlap. Mitigations: stop the etch immediately at the oxide, or use a pulsed DC bias to limit charge buildup.
  • Device layer collapse: Attempts on 2.2µm and 5µm SOI wafers collapsed onto the substrate, visible as interferograms, despite modeling that said they should hold. We suspect residual stress in the thin silicon torques the structure on release.
  • CPD washout: Fluid motion during critical point drying tore suspended structures loose. Guardrails placed 50µm from all device elements helped reduce flow across the surface.

Future Work

  • Improve yield (0.38% in this run) by tuning DRIE to eliminate footing
  • Develop designs that work on less costly 2.2µm or 5µm SOI platforms
  • Explore alternative actuation, such as vertical instead of lateral motion
